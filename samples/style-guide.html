Technical writing style guide — quick reference

Acceptable technical writing is accurate, with a professional level of spelling, grammar, and punctuation. Good technical writing requires more than that. These are the guidelines I apply to every document I write — collected and refined over years of writing technical and instructional content.

I consider every word carefully against these principles, striving to be:

Concise Consistent Clear Complete Empathetic Pleasant to read Adaptive

Be concise

For every word, ask: is it needed? Technical writing is not a school paper with a minimum word count — you don't need filler. However, don't sacrifice clarity on the altar of brevity. Use all the words you need to prevent ambiguity or distortion of meaning.

Watch out for the word of, and for phrases such as "in order to," "you can," and "there are." You often don't need them.

  • Correct: To postpone a delivery, call 1-800-123-4567.
    Incorrect: In order to postpone a delivery, you can call us at 1-800-123-4567.
  • Correct: Several restrictions apply to trial accounts.
    Incorrect: There are several restrictions that apply to trial accounts.
  • Correct: This lets you change the background color.
    Incorrect: This lets you change the color of the background.

Leave marketing copy to the marketing team. Avoid superlatives, excessive claims, clichés, and future tense unless necessary.

  • Correct: This button returns you to your dashboard.
    Incorrect: This button will return you to your dashboard.
  • Correct: Invalid input triggers an error message.
    Incorrect: An error message is triggered by invalid input.

Prefer simple vocabulary. There's no need to use obfuscate if hide will do.

Be consistent

Use a style guide as your baseline standard. If you're the only writer, a style guide still pays off — it ensures consistency for future contributors. Adopt an established guide where one fits (see the links at the bottom of this page), and keep a short list of your own house rules for decisions it doesn't cover.

  • Avoid interchanging synonyms for technical terms. Don't mix directory and folder unless they mean different things.
  • Pick one spelling convention — US or UK English — and do not mix them.
  • Use one word for one UI control, and format it the same way everywhere (for example, always bold the names of buttons).
  • Capitalize headings and UI names by the same rule throughout the document set.

Be clear

  • Use a forward slash for folder paths.
  • Prefer "because" over "as" to indicate causation.
  • Use descriptive link text, not "click here."
  • Ensure a logical information architecture with clear headings and navigation.
  • Avoid e.g. and i.e.; use "for example" or "that is."
  • Place conditions at the start of a sentence:
    Correct: To start over, delete this file.
    Incorrect: Delete this file to start over.
  • Keep related information together. Describe a control beside the step that uses it, not in a paragraph two pages earlier.

Be complete

  • Cover all supported platforms (Android, iOS, Windows, and so on) — or state clearly when a task applies to only one of them.
  • Have reviewers follow your instructions, not just read them.
  • Provide example inputs and outputs, and describe expected error handling.
  • Answer the questions readers will have after finishing the task: what happens next, and where do I go if it didn't work?

Be empathetic

  • Avoid "easily" and "simply." They can feel patronizing if users struggle.
  • Define terms, acronyms, and abbreviations on first use.
  • Use inclusive language. Avoid biased or harmful terms such as master/slave, whitelist/blacklist, dummy, or crazy.
  • Provide diverse examples and avoid cultural assumptions.
  • Avoid colloquialisms — non-native speakers may not understand them.
  • Explain the why behind a step when a reader might hesitate to do it.

Be pleasant to read

  • Vary sentence lengths.
  • Keep sentences under roughly 30 words.
  • Break up long paragraphs to avoid a "wall of text."
  • Prefer lists for anything with more than two items — but keep list items parallel and roughly the same length.

Be adaptive

These guidelines are not absolute — sometimes clarity requires breaking them. Follow your organization's style guide when it differs. Pick your battles, and adapt gracefully.
